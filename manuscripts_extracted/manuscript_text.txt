Abstract
VECTOR is a decentralized micro-credentialing platform designed to address the limitations of traditional academic credential verification systems. Conventional verification methods rely on centralized databases and manual validation procedures, which may lead to inefficiencies, delays, and increased vulnerability to credential fraud. This study presents VECTOR, a hybrid Web 2.5 platform that integrates blockchain technology with artificial intelligence–driven career analytics to provide secure credential issuance, automated verification, and predictive skill analysis.
The system utilizes ERC-1155 smart contracts deployed on the Polygon Amoy Testnet to ensure tamper-resistant credential issuance and decentralized verification. A full-stack web application developed using Next.js and Supabase manages authentication, credential storage, and user interaction. In addition, an artificial intelligence module implemented with Google Gemini and statistical analysis libraries performs skill extraction, detects skill decay trends, and generates personalized course recommendations.
System evaluation was conducted through functional testing, security validation, and performance analysis. Results indicate that the AI skill extraction pipeline achieved an F1-score of 0.61, meeting the defined system performance threshold. Blockchain-based credential issuance and verification were successfully validated through smart contract testing and API-based verification endpoints.The findings demonstrate that integrating blockchain verification with AI-driven analytics can enhance credential authenticity, transparency, and career intelligence for modern digital learning ecosystems.

Chapter One
Introduction
1.1 Background and Problem Statement
The global employment ecosystem is transitioning from traditional credential-based hiring toward competency-based evaluation frameworks. The World Economic Forum (2023) projects that approximately 44% of core workforce skills will undergo disruption within five years due to rapid digital transformation. Static academic qualifications no longer sufficiently represent dynamic labor market competencies. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD, 2022) further reports that nearly one-third of workers are either overqualified or underqualified, reflecting structural misalignment between educational output and industry demand.
In the Philippine context, this misalignment manifests as a dual crisis of unemployment and credential irrelevance. The Philippine Statistics Authority (2025) reports over 845,000 unemployed bachelor’s degree holders, with 43% belonging to the 15–24 age bracket. Simultaneously, sectors such as IT and BPO report skill shortages, indicating that conventional diplomas fail to capture real-time competency signals. The issue is compounded by credential fraud. Industry estimates suggest that approximately 7% of credentials submitted to Philippine employers contain misrepresentations (Avvanz, 2025). Existing verification mechanisms remain manual, resource-intensive, and vulnerable to document manipulation, enabling fraudulent credentials to circulate undetected during early recruitment stages.
Blockchain technology offers cryptographic immutability and decentralized verification. ERC-1155 token standards deployed on Layer-2 networks such as Polygon enable scalable, tamper-proof micro-credential issuance. However, immutability alone does not address skill relevance over time. Artificial Intelligence enables predictive modeling of Skill Decay, correlating credential timestamps with industry demand signals to assess competency obsolescence.
These converging issues establish the need for VECTOR: a Web 2.5 Hybrid decentralized micro-credentialing system integrating blockchain immutability with predictive AI-driven career analytics to transform static academic records into dynamic, verifiable skill portfolios.
​Statement of the Problem
​Traditional academic credentialing systems are static, vulnerable to fraud, and incapable of reflecting evolving labor market demands. Employers lack trustless, real-time verification mechanisms, while graduates lack tools to monitor skill relevance and anticipate competency obsolescence. This study aims to design and implement VECTOR, a decentralized micro-credentialing system that enables tamper-proof credential issuance and predictive Skill Decay detection.
​        Specifically, this study aims to answer the following questions:
How can a Web 2.5 Hybrid Architecture utilizing Polygon-based ERC-1155 tokens enhance credential reliability and integrity?
How effective is a Hybrid AI Pipeline combining Gemini 2.5 Flash NLP and a deterministic 3-signal Weighted Velocity Scoring model in detecting Skill Decay?
​What is the Precision@5 performance of a Cosine Similarity-based Recommendation Engine in generating relevant upskilling pathways?
To what extent does a QR-linked Credential Verification Repository improve verification efficiency compared to traditional manual processes?
1.2.1 General Objectives
        
The general objective of this study is to design, develop, and implement VECTOR: A Decentralized Micro-Credentialing System with Predictive Career Analytics and Skill Decay Detection, integrating blockchain-based immutability with AI-driven competency forecasting.
1.2.2 Specific Objectives
The specific objectives of the project are the following, arranged in chronological order based on the software development process:
To analyze registrar and HR credential verification workflows and identify systemic gaps in fraud prevention and continuous skill tracking.
To design a modular Web 2.5 Hybrid Architecture integrating a blockchain layer, AI analytics engine, and role-based dashboards.
To develop and deploy ERC-1155 smart contracts on the Polygon Amoy Testnet for tamper-proof micro-credential issuance.
To implement a Hybrid AI Pipeline utilizing Gemini 2.5 Flash for zero-shot skill normalization and a deterministic 3-signal Weighted Velocity Scoring model (trend slope 40%, job volume 30%, recency 30%) for Skill Decay detection.
To implement a Cosine Similarity-based recommendation engine (ml-matrix) with Tier 1/Tier 2 domain filtering, targeting Precision@5 ≥ 0.70.
To generate a QR-linked Credential Verification Repository (CVR) enabling trustless employer authentication.
To evaluate AI performance (F1 ≥ 0.60 baseline), blockchain minting latency, and system compliance with ISO/IEC 25010:2023 quality characteristics.
1.3 Platform Choice Justification
The VECTOR platform adopts a Web 2.5 Hybrid Architecture consisting of:
Polygon Layer-2 blockchain (ERC-1155) for credential immutability
Next.js serverless application layer as an Off-Chain Mirror
Supabase (PostgreSQL) for off-chain metadata and Skill Health Cache
Google Gemini 2.5 Flash for zero-shot NLP normalization
        Gemini 2.5 Flash is strictly confined to skill extraction and normalization of unstructured credential text. Quantitative forecasting is executed using a deterministic Weighted Velocity Scoring model combining trend slope (40%), job volume (30%), and recency (30%), producing a bounded velocityScore ∈ [0,100].
ARIMA forecasting was formally evaluated but deferred due to insufficient temporal density within the market_snapshots dataset. A minimum accumulation period of 6–12 months of daily snapshots is required before ARIMA achieves statistical validity. This principled deferral prevents unreliable predictive outputs during the prototype phase.
Cosine Similarity (ml-matrix) is selected for recommendation generation due to its deterministic behavior and empirically validated performance in course recommendation systems.
1.4 Significance of the Project
The development of 
VECTOR
 holds significant value for the following stakeholders:
For the Technology Industry:
 This project demonstrates the practical application of decentralized applications (dApps) beyond financial use cases. It serves as a proof-of-concept for using ERC-1155 tokens to manage intellectual property and verified records, contributing to the broader adoption of Web3 architecture in enterprise data management.

For Students and Alumni:
 It grants individuals "Self-Sovereign Identity" (SSI) over their achievements. The AI integration further adds value by providing data-driven alerts on when their skills are becoming obsolete, enabling proactive upskilling to maintain market relevance.

1.5.2 Limitations
Network Dependency:
 The system functions rely on the uptime and latency of the Polygon Amoy Testnet and public RPC nodes; network congestion may result in delayed transaction confirmations.
Test Environment:
 As a prototype, the system operates on a testnet environment using test currency (MATIC/POL). Real-world financial costs of minting on the Mainnet are calculated but not incurred during the study.
AI Data Constraints:
 The "Career Coach" and "Skill Decay" predictions are limited to the dataset provided during the training/configuration phase and do not pull live, real-time job market data from the open internet during the pilot.
Browser Compatibility:
 The application requires a Web3-enabled browser environment (or extensions like MetaMask) for the Registrar and Student roles to sign transactions; it is not designed as a native mobile application.
Recommendation Cold Start:
 The Collaborative Filtering engine relies on existing peer enrollment data to generate suggestions. Consequently, initial recommendations for early users may rely solely on content-based matching until sufficient enrollment data is populated within the system.

2.2 Sources of Data

API responses are validated against the internal reference dataset before influencing skill health scores, providing an additional accuracy safeguard.

2.6 Security Implementation
Security was a fundamental design consideration in the development of the VECTOR platform due to the sensitive nature of academic records and the irreversible characteristics of blockchain-based credential issuance. The system adopts a layered security approach that combines blockchain-level protections, application-layer safeguards, secure data handling practices, and controlled AI service integration to ensure confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity across all operations.
At the blockchain level, ERC-1155 smart contracts were designed and implemented following established best practices to prevent common vulnerabilities such as unauthorized minting, improper access control, and transaction manipulation. Credential minting is restricted to authorized registrar wallets through server-side authorization checks, ensuring that only legitimate institutional actors can issue credentials. Once minted, credentials are cryptographically signed and recorded on the Polygon blockchain, providing immutability and verifiable provenance that prevents post-issuance alteration or forgery.
Within the application layer, security mechanisms were implemented to protect user interactions and backend processes. Input validation is enforced on both client-side and server-side components to mitigate risks such as malformed data submission and injection attacks. Authentication and role-based access control are managed through Supabase, ensuring that registrars, students, and third-party verifiers have access only to functionalities relevant to their roles. All serverless API communications operate over secure HTTPS channels with token-based authorization to prevent unauthorized access and data interception.
Data privacy and storage security were also prioritized through the separation of on-chain and off-chain data. Sensitive academic metadata and user profiles are stored off-chain within Supabase, protected by access control policies and database-level security configurations. Supporting documents and credential metadata stored on IPFS via Pinata utilize content-addressable hashes, ensuring that publicly accessible verification materials cannot be altered without detection. Additionally, interactions with the AI Career Coach are conducted using anonymized or minimally scoped data to reduce exposure of personally identifiable information during analytics processing.
Collectively, these security measures provide a robust framework that safeguards academic credentials against fraud, unauthorized access, and data tampering, while preserving the transparency and trust advantages inherent to decentralized systems.

4.1 Interpretation of Results
The results presented in Chapter 3 demonstrate that the VECTOR platform successfully integrates blockchain-based credential verification with AI-driven career analytics within a unified system architecture. The observed evaluation metrics provide evidence that the implemented subsystems namely the AI extraction module, blockchain credential infrastructure, and web application layer operate cohesively to achieve the objectives defined in Chapter 1.
The AI extraction component achieved a measured F1 score of 0.61 with precision and recall values of 0.62 and 0.60 respectively. These values exceed the minimum performance threshold established during system design, indicating that the extraction mechanism is capable of reliably identifying skill entities from heterogeneous credential text. While the achieved accuracy does not yet reach the level of specialized machine-learning models trained on large domain-specific datasets, it demonstrates that the hybrid extraction strategy employed in the system is sufficiently effective for early-stage career analytics. In practice, the extraction pipeline provides meaningful structured inputs for subsequent modules such as skill health forecasting and course recommendation generation.
The blockchain subsystem also demonstrated reliable operation during testing. Credential issuance through ERC-1155 smart contracts deployed on the Polygon Amoy test network successfully produced verifiable token records associated with student wallets. The verification APIs were able to independently query blockchain state and confirm token ownership using JSON-RPC calls. This behavior validates the system’s decentralized verification approach, where credential authenticity can be confirmed without reliance on centralized databases. Such architecture improves transparency and reduces the risk of credential tampering or falsification.
At the system level, the platform demonstrated stable end-to-end workflow execution. Registrars were able to issue credentials, students were able to view and analyze their verified skills, and external parties were able to perform public credential verification. The successful interaction between the web portal, blockchain infrastructure, AI engine, and relational database confirms that the proposed modular architecture effectively supports the intended functionality of the platform.Overall, the observed results suggest that the VECTOR platform provides a technically feasible solution for integrating decentralized credential verification with intelligent career analytics, fulfilling the design objectives established in the earlier chapters of this study.
4.2 Challenges and Solutions
The development of the VECTOR platform involved several technical challenges associated with integrating heterogeneous technologies including blockchain infrastructure, artificial intelligence processing, and secure web application frameworks.One major challenge involved the synchronization of blockchain operations with traditional web application workflows. Unlike conventional database transactions, blockchain interactions require asynchronous confirmation through distributed network consensus. This introduces latency and uncertainty into application logic. To address this challenge, the researchers implemented transaction monitoring mechanisms through Ethers.js and designed the registrar dashboard to handle wallet-based signing operations via MetaMask. The system architecture separates blockchain confirmation from database persistence, ensuring that credential records are stored only after successful transaction verification.
Another significant challenge involved processing credential text with varying structure and terminology. Academic certificates, training credentials, and professional certifications often describe skills using inconsistent vocabulary. This variability complicates automated skill extraction processes. To mitigate this issue, the AI pipeline incorporated normalization routines and evaluation against a manually curated golden dataset. This evaluation process allowed the researchers to calibrate extraction behavior and measure accuracy using precision, recall, and F1 metrics.
Security considerations also introduced development complexity. Because the platform exposes public verification endpoints and administrative credential issuance routes, strong input validation and access control mechanisms were required. The system therefore incorporates multiple security layers including role-based access control, schema validation using Zod, request rate limiting, encrypted storage of sensitive notes, and authentication verification workflows. These measures collectively reduce the risk of injection attacks, unauthorized credential manipulation, and data exposure.
Integration complexity represented another development challenge. The platform combines several subsystems including blockchain smart contracts, an AI analysis engine, and a Next.js-based web application. Coordinating these components required a modular design approach in which each subsystem operates independently but communicates through well-defined API routes. The monorepo architecture used in the project allowed shared dependencies and consistent configuration across modules, significantly simplifying integration management.Through these solutions, the researchers were able to overcome the principal technical challenges encountered during system development.
4.3 Limitations and Improvements
Despite the successful implementation of the VECTOR platform, several limitations were identified during testing and evaluation.One limitation relates to the accuracy of the AI extraction module. While the measured F1 score of 0.61 satisfies the system’s minimum performance requirement, it indicates that the model may still miss certain skills or incorrectly classify ambiguous text segments. This limitation is primarily due to the relatively small dataset used during evaluation. Future improvements could involve training specialized machine-learning models on larger domain-specific datasets to improve extraction accuracy and reduce ambiguity in skill detection.
A second limitation concerns blockchain testing conditions. All blockchain interactions were performed on the Polygon Amoy test network rather than a production environment. Although the test network accurately simulates blockchain functionality, real-world deployment would introduce additional variables such as transaction fees, network congestion, and higher operational traffic. Additional testing under production conditions would therefore be necessary to evaluate long-term system performance.
Another limitation involves the scope of market intelligence data used for career analytics. The current implementation relies on periodic snapshots of job market data to estimate skill demand trends. While this provides useful insights, more comprehensive datasets could significantly improve the accuracy of skill health forecasting and course recommendation algorithms. Integrating additional labor market sources or real-time data feeds could enhance the predictive capabilities of the platform.
The user interface also represents an area for potential improvement. Although the current interface supports all core system workflows, additional usability studies could identify opportunities to improve accessibility, visualization clarity, and interaction efficiency for students and registrars.Addressing these limitations would further strengthen the reliability and scalability of the VECTOR platform in future implementations.
4.4 Implications and Future Work
The development of VECTOR demonstrates the potential benefits of combining decentralized credential verification with AI-driven analytics within digital education ecosystems.Blockchain-based credential systems have the potential to significantly improve trust and transparency in academic record management. By storing credential proofs on a decentralized ledger, institutions can provide tamper-resistant verification mechanisms that reduce administrative overhead and minimize fraud risks. Such systems could simplify credential validation for employers and training institutions while providing students with portable, verifiable records of achievement.
The integration of AI analytics further enhances the value of digital credential platforms. By analyzing skill trends and labor market signals, systems like VECTOR can provide personalized insights that support career planning and continuous learning. This capability could help students identify emerging skill gaps and make informed decisions regarding professional development.Future development of the platform could focus on expanding the scale and accuracy of market intelligence integration. Incorporating additional job market datasets, training catalogs, and industry certification frameworks could significantly improve the quality of recommendation outputs.
Another potential direction involves interoperability with decentralized identity standards and global digital credential frameworks. Integrating decentralized identifiers (DIDs) and verifiable credential standards could allow the platform to operate across institutions, enabling broader adoption beyond a single academic environment.Finally, real-world deployment in educational institutions would provide valuable insights regarding system usability, scalability, and adoption barriers. Such deployment would allow further refinement of both the blockchain credential infrastructure and the AI analytics components.

This study presented the design and implementation of VECTOR, a decentralized micro-credentialing and career analytics platform that integrates blockchain-based credential verification with artificial intelligence–driven skill analysis. The system was developed to address limitations in traditional credential verification systems, which often rely on centralized databases and manual validation procedures that may be vulnerable to fraud or inefficiency.
The implemented platform combines several technological components including a full-stack web application, a blockchain credential infrastructure built on ERC-1155 smart contracts, and an AI engine capable of analyzing skill trends and generating career recommendations.
